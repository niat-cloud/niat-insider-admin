Introduction

Six months ago I walked in at orientation with no idea what I was doing.

I have now been here long enough to say what is genuinely good about this place and what is genuinely hard about it — and both lists are real. If you are deciding whether to come here, the second list probably matters more to you than the first.

The Support System Is Real, Not Marketing

The instructors and mentors here are not the distant professors I had heard about from friends at other colleges. They are young, approachable, and actually good at what they do.

In my first meeting with my success coach, she told me I could share anything that was worrying me — no judgement. That one conversation made me feel like somebody was genuinely invested in whether I succeeded or failed.

And it is not just talk. When I got stuck on a project, they showed up. When I was stressed about how fast everything moves, they listened. Having people who care about your growth changes how you approach a problem, because you stop trying to solve everything alone.

What We Actually Learn

The curriculum is built around what the industry needs now, not what was relevant five years ago.

We do not spend time on disconnected theory. Every subject feels like it is there for a reason, because it connects to real work.

I have lost count of the workshops and hackathons. There was a hackathon, then an ideathon, then a buildathon — and each one made me think about problem-solving differently. These are not resume-padding exercises. You learn to build something, work under pressure, and start again when your first idea does not survive contact with reality.

The biggest thing I understood in six months had nothing to do with code or frameworks. It was this: college is not about completing courses. It is about building the ability to learn anything when you need it.

The Reality Check — What Is Hard Here

This place does not feel like a typical college, and honestly that cuts both ways.

The pace is fast. Really fast. New concepts come at you every week and you are expected to keep up. Attendance is not optional. Procrastination is not an option. If you are someone who works better with flexibility, your first few weeks will be rough.

  • The pace demands genuine time-management skills
  • Attendance expectations are strict — for good reason, but strict
  • You will be juggling academics, projects and personal growth at the same time

The other thing I noticed: events and celebrations here are not as vibrant as what I have heard about from other NIAT campuses. Being at the NRI campus comes with certain restrictions from the university side. The team tries their best to keep things happening, but it is not quite the same energy. That is just how it is.

The Advantage Nobody Mentions

Here is something I did not expect to appreciate until semester exams came around.

We are only tested on the subjects we actually study. There is no random physics or chemistry requirement to memorise despite it having nothing to do with our specialisation. That alone saves weeks of wasted effort compared with students at other colleges — and I only understood how big a deal it was when I watched friends elsewhere revising things they would never use.

Conclusion

Looking back at these six months, I am not the same person who walked in at orientation.

I have built projects I am actually proud of. I communicate better with the people I work with. And I have got comfortable being uncomfortable, which is more or less the definition of growth. There is a long road ahead, but I am genuinely interested to see where it goes — at NIAT and after it.
