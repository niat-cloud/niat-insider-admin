I'm going to be honest with you — my first month at NIAT was nothing like I imagined it would be. It was messier, louder, more overwhelming, and somehow also more energizing than anything I had mentally prepared for. Here's what those thirty days actually looked like.

Week One: Finding Rooms and Questioning My Choice

The first week felt like someone handed me a puzzle with all the pieces but no picture on the box. Orientation sessions, unfamiliar faces everywhere, a schedule that looked like a corporate spreadsheet — it hit all at once and there was no easing into it.

Here's the thing nobody warns you about though: everyone around you is equally lost. That shared confusion becomes the first thing that connects you to people. Our batch WhatsApp group went from zero to 247 unread messages within 48 hours. Roughly half of them were "guys where is the lab?" and the other half were "what's the dress code tomorrow?" Nobody knew. Everyone was figuring it out together.

That collective cluelessness is actually where the first friendships form. Not in any planned icebreaker activity — in the chaos of week one, trying to locate a classroom before the session starts.

Practical note: save the campus map on your phone before day one. I cannot stress this enough.

Week Two: A Familiar Lab Desk and a Faster Pace

By the second week something shifted. Not dramatically, but enough to notice. I knew which chai stall opened earliest. I had silently claimed a desk in the lab that was now understood to be mine. I had maybe three friends who had genuinely stuck around past the polite introductory phase.

The coursework also picked up pace noticeably. The trainers made it clear early that this wasn't going to be a gradual build — NIAT moves at a real speed and the expectation is that you move with it.

What actually surprised me in week two was the energy in the rooms. People here are building things, not just reading about them. There's a difference between a place that talks about practical learning and a place where you walk into a lab and everyone around you is genuinely working on something. NIAT is the second kind. That was the week I stopped wondering if I'd made the right choice.

Weeks Three and Four: Peer Learning and My First Presentation

By week three I had completely stopped tracking which day of the month it was. Not because I was comfortable — because I was genuinely too occupied to notice.

Projects, peer learning sessions, the kind of late-night "bhai ek cheez samjha" calls that become their own category of social interaction — it had become a whole lifestyle rather than just an academic schedule. I gave my first internal presentation during this stretch, completely lost the thread in the opening thirty seconds, somehow recovered, and walked out with a response that was better than I deserved. That's probably the most honest description of early growth I can offer.

What genuinely caught me off guard was how much my batchmates pushed me forward. Not in a competitive way — in a genuinely collaborative one. I learned more from late-night study sessions in those two weeks than I could have figured out alone in a month. NIAT provides the structure and the content. For me, my people provided the momentum that carried me through.

The person sitting next to you in the lab has probably already spent two hours on the exact problem you just started googling. Just ask. Everyone here is figuring things out and most people are happy to figure them out together.

What I Think About the First Month Now

Completely. Without hesitation.

It was messy and intense and occasionally overwhelming in ways I didn't anticipate. There were moments in week one where I genuinely questioned whether I was cut out for the pace. There were late nights in week three where I was running on chai and stubbornness and not much else.

But those thirty days were also some of the most genuinely energizing I've had. I began finding my rhythm and the people who stayed beyond the first introductions—the ones I could call at 11 PM when something was not making sense. That gave me a reason to keep going.

Just give it the thirty days. The puzzle starts making sense — even without the box.
